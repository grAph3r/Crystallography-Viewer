The determination of the interface structure between ionocovalent
compounds: the general case study of the Al 2O3/ZrO 2large mis-fit system
G. Trolliard, *aR. Benmechta,aD. Mercurioaand O. I. Lebedevb
Received 22nd March 2006, Accepted 25th July 2006
First published as an Advance Article on the web 23rd August 2006
DOI: 10.1039/b604232e
Heterophase interfaces between alumina and zirconia are investigated by an HREM approach.
The studied samples are synthesized by the sol–gel route, which allows the growth of thin islands
of pure zirconia showing heteroepitaxial growth on (112 ¯0)sasapphire substrates. Their
heteroepitaxial relationship is first established by electron diffraction. Specific cross sectional
samples are then prepared, normal to the in-plane common directions. Then, HREM images
allow definition of the metric of the coincidence super-cell between the two lattices. The crystal
chemistry of these heteroepitaxial interfaces is then presented for two peculiar orientation
variants. The geometry of the interfacial polyhedra of coordination is presented and a 3D
interface model is established for the main orientation. It is shown that the orientation
relationship is governed by the occurrence of continuous cationic lattice planes across the
interface. They correspond to ‘structural walls’ which are typical structural features of the
orientation variants.
Alumina/zirconia represents a case of large mis-fit system. However, except for the expected
geometrical dislocations and ledges, the interfaces present very few physical defects. However, the
overall interfacial polyhedra of coordination suffer high distortion. The lattice mismatch could
thus be locally accommodated by an important elastic strain linked to fluctuations of the cation–
anion interatomic distances. The predominance of this relaxation mechanism, based on the
adaptability of the ionocovalent bonds, seems a characteristic of oxide/oxide interfaces.
Introduction
When two compounds present heteroepitaxial relationships,
the interface between them exhibits a specific structure. It’s
investigation at the atomic scale is, however, not easy as this
crystalline region is actually perturbed by a large amount of
defects and suffers high lattice strain, mainly due to lattice
mismatches. In addition, owing to its 2-dimensional character,
the diffracting volume is so restricted that XRD investigations
can only provide limited information. Thus, TEM remains one
of the most appropriate techniques to determine the structure
of interfaces. Based on TEM analysis, there are certain cases in
which the interface structure is well understood. The deter-
mination of the structure of interfaces has been particularly
successful in metals1,2and in covalent solids, such as semi-
conductors for example.3–8The determination of the coin-
cidence site lattice (CSL) represents a particular structure of
the interface, and calculations conducted by lowering the
interfacial energy provide more stable orientations. The
expected results can be confirmed by TEM investigations,
and when the coincidence relationships are not perfect, near
coincidence site lattice (NCSL) calculations are carried out in
order to get the best structural fit. If the lattice mismatch is toohigh, depending on the relative parameters or film thickness
(critical thickness), the lattice strain is usually relaxed by
interfacial defects1,3and in such a case TEM analysis provides
complete set of structural information both to build up an
interface model and to understand the mechanisms which
accommodate the mismatch between the two lattices.4In
addition, chemical information has recently been provided in
covalent compounds by determining the direct compositional
analysis of AlGaAs/GaAs heterostructures.9
Perovskite-type compounds are functional oxides and with
this aim, were intensively investigated by TEM as thin films
deposited on perovskite single crystals10–14and recently as
superlattices15exhibiting dual properties. In all these materials,
a structural continuity across the interface is obviously
achieved by corner-sharing octahedra16,17to maintain the
perovskite stacking across the interface. The interface model
is thus easily drawn and it may be, due to these structural
similarities, that the relaxation mechanisms involved in these
systems are very close to those observed in metals or covalent
compounds, as stated by Langjahr et al.18Detailed investiga-
tions of the chemical composition in the nanoscale regions by
EELS are reported to help a better understanding of the
electrical properties of perovskite multilayer systems.19
At the moment, very few works deal with the establishment
of the interface structures in heteroepitaxial oxides exhibiting
contrasted structural properties.20,21Up to now, in the
alumina/zirconia system, some of the orientation variants
have been successfully established either in the case of thin

Conclusion
The studied alumina/zirconia interfaces are large mis-fit
systems. The HREM study, however, reveals that they often
display very strong and simple coincidence relationships. We
show that the establishment of the in-plane orientation of thestudied islands is due to the occurrence of regular and
continuous cationic lattice planes across the interface. They
correspond to ‘structural walls’ which are typical structural
features of the orientation variants of the zirconia islands
grown on [112 ¯0]
sasapphire substrate. Their periodicity is
determined by HREM image analysis in order to define the
coincidence super-cell between the two lattices. Based on thisinformation, structural models can be drawn, designing the
specific geometry of the interface polyhedra. The interface is a
layer of zirconium atoms in a 7-fold coordination. This
coordination results in the specific geometry of the two anionic
lattices of the surrounding phases: 3
6oxygen lattice in alumina
and 44oxygen lattice in zirconia. The interfacial polyhedra are
thus characterized by an average coordination, comprisedbetween the 6-fold coordination of aluminium in sapphire and
the 8-fold coordination of zirconium in the high temperature
tetragonal zirconia.
For the main orientation variant, interface models were
determined both for the tetragonal phase characteristic of the
high temperature form of zirconia and for the monoclinic onethat represents the low temperature form. In such a case,
the heteroepitaxial relationship seems consistent with the
martensitic phase transformation occurring within zirconia
crystals during the cooling of the sample. In the opposite, for
the secondary variant no realistic interface model could be
established for the tetragonal phase. We will show in a
forthcoming paper that the interfacial relationship stronglyinfluences the symmetry of the growing zirconia phase and we
must consider that zirconia islands can directly grow into the
monoclinic form even at elevated temperature (1500 uC).
Owing to the large difference between the two coupled
structures, building the interface supposes a strong structural
recovering, especially due to a great angular mis-fit. In a firstapproach, the regulation mechanism can obviously be depicted
by a periodical insertion of mis-fit edge dislocations in one of
the compounds. We suggest that the ‘structural walls’ play
a key role in the lattice strain accommodation, especially
by slight displacement of the zirconium atoms within theinterfacial polyhedra. These atomic shifts, located within the
interfacial area, account for the periodical occurrence of the‘structural walls’. The lattice strain accommodation withinthe interface is however not limited to the occurrence of these‘structural walls’. Between them, all the interfacial polyhedrasuffer high distortion based on adjustment of their interatomicdistances. The lattice mismatch could thus be locally accom-modated due to the softness of the anionic lattice and,generally speaking, to the adaptability of the ionocovalentbonds. This relaxation mechanism, typical in oxides, enlight-ens the crucial role of the chemical bonds in the formation ofthe heterophase interfaces.
